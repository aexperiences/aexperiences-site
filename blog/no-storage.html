Why We Won’t Sell You Storage

We priced a drive last week. Then I broke my own price in about ten minutes and killed the product. Here is the arithmetic that killed it, and the thing worth building instead.
Anthony EspositoSeptember 13, 20264 min read

We have a server sitting on a shelf that costs us nothing to run, and last week I asked the obvious question. Could it hold files? Could we sell a drive?

The answer came back with a price on it: forty-nine dollars a month, flat, no per-seat fee, as much room as a small company could reasonably use. On paper that looked like a product.

So I did what we do with every price before it goes on a shelf. I went and looked at what the other guy charges.

It took about ten minutes to break it.

The arithmetic

Microsoft will sell a small business a terabyte per person for seven dollars a user a month — and hand over Word, Excel, Outlook and Teams in the same breath. Dropbox starts at fifteen a user. Proton will give you a couple hundred gigabytes and a promise that they cannot read a byte of it even if they wanted to.

Run our forty-nine dollars against that. A three-person shop pays Microsoft twenty-one dollars and gets the entire office suite with it. We would have been more than twice the price for a folder.

Go the other way and it gets worse, not better. At twenty people our flat price wins on paper — and it still loses, because that company is not going to cancel Microsoft. They need the email and the spreadsheets. All we would have done is add a second bill to a company that already had the first one.

That is the whole test, and we failed it both directions.

Nobody actually sells gigabytes

Then I saw the shape of the thing. Every one of those companies is paying for that storage out of a different pocket.

  • Microsoft funds it with Office.
  • Google funds it with advertising.
  • Proton funds it with privacy.
  • Apple funds it with hardware.
  • Dropbox funds it with seats — the one thing we will never charge you for.

Storage is not the product anywhere. It is the thing they hand you so that you stay standing next to the thing they actually sell.

We do not have a different pocket. We would have walked in last and walked in highest, funding a giveaway out of nothing. That is not a business. That is a bill with our name on it.

So I killed it. No storage tiers. Nothing on the shelf that sells you gigabytes.

Nobody makes money selling you gigabytes. They make money selling you the reason you needed them.

What survived

Here is the part I did not throw away.

Every one of those companies sells you a folder, and not one of them knows what is inside it. Dropbox cannot tell a work order from a grocery list. Microsoft does not know which of those files is a patient’s chart. Proton cannot know by design — that is the entire point of Proton, and it is a good one.

None of them can know, because none of them owns the system the work actually happens in.

We own fourteen.

When a set of drawings lands in Buttress OS, the system already knows the project, the phase, the sheet number, and whose desk has to clear it. When a visit note lands in Stay@Home OS, it is already tied to the client, the caregiver and the invoice that has not been sent yet. Nobody has to name the file correctly. Nobody has to remember which folder it belongs in. It is not in a folder. It is on the record.

That is not storage. That is the file layer of an operating system, and it only works if you own the operating system.

So AE Drive is not going on the shelf as a product. It is going inside the fourteen, included, the way everything else is included. No per-seat pricing. No build fee on the license. Not now, not ever.

The two things they cannot copy

I nearly wrote the whole idea off, and that would have been the wrong call, because there are exactly two things we can do here that the big drives structurally cannot.

The first is sending files out. Most of them meter delivery, because moving data costs them money. The shelf we are already standing on does not charge us that way, which means handing a client a finished set of files can be part of the product instead of a line on a meter. A delivery engine, not a locker.

The second is that the system that filed it can read it. Not a search box that matches a word in a file name — the operating system already knows that this is the structural set for phase three, and it can act on that. The people selling folders cannot do that, and the one company built on never reading your files least of all.

What it cost to find out

A week, and a price I had to throw away.

That is how it is supposed to work here. Every project starts as an estimate, including the ones we build for ourselves, and the estimate exists so it can kill things. This one did its job. It cost a week to learn that the product was a bill, and it saved us from putting a shelf of gigabytes in a store where everything else is an operating system.

The best thing an estimate ever does is tell you no.
